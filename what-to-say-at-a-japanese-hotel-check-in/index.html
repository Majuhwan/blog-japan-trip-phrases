What to Say at a Japanese Hotel Check-In

Practical check-in and room-related Japanese phrases taken from JNTO's official traveler handbook, covering reservations, room requests, and ryokan stays.

Japan Trip PhrasesOct 9, 2026 · 4 min read

The Japan National Tourism Organization (JNTO), Japan's official tourism body, publishes a Tourist's Language Handbook specifically designed for situations like hotel check-in, built around a point-and-show format: travelers can point to their question in English, and the person helping them can point to the matching Japanese answer. The handbook's accommodation section covers exactly the phrases and situations a traveler actually runs into at check-in, check-out, and during a stay.

Arriving with (or without) a reservation

If you already have a booking, JNTO's handbook gives this line for arriving at the front desk:

予約してあります_____です。チェックインをお願いします。 (My name is _____. I have a reservation. I'd like to check in, please.)

If you arrive without a booking, the handbook provides a direct way to ask whether a room is still available:

予約してないのですが、今晩_____人用の部屋はありますか。 (I have no reservation tonight, but do you have a room for _____ person(s)?)

The handbook also includes the front desk's likely response pattern, which is useful to recognize even if you can't produce it yourself: はい。一人1泊（素泊まり／朝食付き）_____円です, meaning "Yes, it's _____ yen per person per night," with a distinction between 素泊まり (room only, no meals) and 朝食付き (with breakfast included). Knowing that distinction matters because room-only and breakfast-included rates at the same hotel can differ meaningfully, and it's easy to miss which one a quoted price refers to.

Asking about the room itself

Before settling in, JNTO's handbook includes several practical requests:

  • 禁煙（フロア／ルーム）はありますか。 (Do you have any non-smoking floors/rooms?)
  • 部屋を見せていただけますか。 (May I see the room?)
  • すぐに出かけたいので荷物を預かってください。 (Since I'd like to go out right now, please keep my baggage.)

That last phrase is specifically useful for the common situation of arriving before official check-in time but wanting to drop bags and start exploring immediately.

Requesting a room change

If something is wrong with the assigned room, the handbook gives a direct template: 　_____ので部屋を変えてください (please change my room because _____), with a list of common reasons already translated and ready to slot in, including:

  • 部屋が汚れている (it is dirty)
  • 部屋が狭い (it is too small)
  • （外／隣）がうるさい (it is noisy outside/in the next room)
  • 部屋に（お風呂／トイレ）がない (there is no bathroom/toilet)
  • 部屋が（暑い／寒い） (it is hot/cold)
  • 景色が良くない (the view is not good)

Having these ready to point to removes the need to describe a problem from scratch in an unfamiliar language under a bit of stress.

Practical requests during a stay

The handbook also covers everyday logistics that come up mid-stay: asking whether internet or Wi-Fi is available (インターネットが使えますか？ / Wi-Fiは使えますか？), asking to extend a stay by additional nights (もう_____泊延泊できますか), confirming whether you can pay by credit card (クレジットカードで支払えますか), requesting a taxi (タクシーを呼んでもらえますか), and asking the front desk to forward luggage ahead to an airport or your next accommodation.

Staying at a ryokan specifically

For a traditional Japanese inn (ryokan) rather than a standard hotel, JNTO's handbook includes a short, separate set of phrases tailored to that style of stay: asking whether meals can be eaten in your room (食事は部屋でできますか？), asking about buying a yukata (cotton kimono) as a souvenir (おみやげに浴衣を買いたいのですが), and asking whether a sweet served in the room is available for purchase (部屋で食べたお菓子は売っていますか？). These reflect the fact that a ryokan stay often includes in-room meal service and amenities a standard hotel does not, which is why the phrasebook treats it as a distinct section rather than folding it into general hotel check-in.

A few universal phrases worth knowing regardless of hotel type

JNTO's separate, general Japanese phrase list for travelers includes a short set of greetings and courtesy phrases that pair well with any check-in interaction: Sumimasen (excuse me) to open a request politely, Arigatou (thank you) to close it, and Wakarimasen (I don't understand) for when a reply comes back faster or more complex than expected.

Key takeaways

  • JNTO's official Tourist's Language Handbook gives a direct check-in phrase for both reserved and walk-in arrivals, plus the likely front-desk reply pattern distinguishing room-only versus breakfast-included rates.
  • A short set of ready-made room-change reasons (too small, too noisy, too hot or cold, no bathroom, bad view) means you don't have to improvise a description under pressure.
  • Mid-stay logistics like Wi-Fi access, extending your stay, paying by credit card, and requesting a taxi all have direct phrases in the same handbook section.
  • Ryokan stays have their own small set of phrases covering in-room meals and yukata purchases, reflecting how different that style of stay is from a standard hotel.
  • Pairing any request with Sumimasen to open and Arigatou to close goes a long way even when the rest of the sentence doesn't come out perfectly.

The handbook is built to be pointed at, not memorized, which makes it worth having open on a phone or printed at the front desk the first time any of these situations comes up.
